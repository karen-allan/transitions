Facts about dying * In 1920–1922 life expectancy in Canada for men in was 58.8 years, for women, 60.6 years.^1 * In 2017, life expectancy for men in Canada was 80.0 years, for women, 84.1 years.^2 * In 1900 USA, the top causes of death were pneumonia, flu, tb, gi infections and diphtheria.^3 * People were cared for by family, dying at home with neighbors and friends paying their respects.^4 * With families moving to cities, funerals/wakes were handed over to the emerging funeral industry.^5 * With advances in hygiene and sanitation, antibiotics and vaccines, people put faith in medicine.^6 * In 1930, just 30% of Canadians and Americans died in hospital; by 1994, 77.3% died in hospital.^7 * With strict visiting rules up until 2000, including a ban on children visiting, many patients died alone.^8 * While death from accidents and infections have decreased, chronic disease now kills 70% of us.^9 * A recent study show that most doctors graduate without even basic competencies in palliative care.^10 * 75% of Canadians surveyed said that they want to die at home while in reality only 15% died at home.^11 * Only 16 to 30% of dying Canadians can access and/or receive hospice, palliative or end-of-life care.^12 * In 2007 the Canadian government disbanded the Secretariat on Palliative and End-of-Life Care.^13 * Under the Canada Health Act, home and continuing care are not covered as insurable services.^14 * Unpaid Canadian caregivers provided $9 billion of care in 2019, estimating to $27 billion by 2050.^15 * Chronic disease rates are increasing faster among those aged 35-64 years than those 65 years plus.^16 * Almost 60% died in hospitals after receiving pointless, often stressful measures to prevent death.^16 * Review: End-of-life Doulas offer a promising way to ‘suture’ the division of health and death care.^16 * Review: End-of-life carers may represent a new direction for the dying to personalize their care.^16
